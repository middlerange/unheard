## POST STRUCTURE (3-block format)

**Block 1:Frame the problem (1–2 lines)**
Clear, specific, relatable. Start with tension, not context. Skip context-setting entirely.

**Block 2:Expand the insight (3–5 lines)**
Show what's actually happening, why it matters, what people miss. Contrast works well here.

**Block 3:Implication or direction (1–2 lines)**
A shift in thinking, a new lens, or a soft directional takeaway. Not a prescription unless it's tactical. Never a product pitch.

## OPENING PATTERNS

**The Misconception Hook**
Name what people believe → show why it fails.
> Most teams think shipping fast is the advantage.
> Speed without alignment quietly kills adoption.

**The Recognition Hook**
Describe a familiar friction state. Immediate pattern recognition, no buildup.
> Features ship daily. Communication still runs weekly.

**The Number Hook**
Lead with a specific, surprising data point. No framing needed:the number is the hook.

---

## SENTENCE PATTERNS

**Contrast pair**:old way vs real outcome:
> Activity looks like progress. Adoption creates progress.

**Question bridge**:pose doubt, answer immediately:
> Isn't faster shipping enough? Not if no one understands what changed.

**Short-long rhythm**:keeps posts readable:
> Teams ship constantly. But without a shared source of truth across marketing, sales, and support, the value of those releases never compounds. So the work resets every time.

---

## CLOSING PATTERNS

**Insight close:**
> The bottleneck didn't disappear. It just moved.

**Direction close:**
> Speed matters. Shared understanding matters more.

**Soft solution bridge (use sparingly):**
> This is why release communication needs structure, not reminders.

## DOMAIN KNOWLEDGE (weave in naturally:never explain like a textbook)

- **Communication debt**: the gap between what shipped and what users know about. Compounds like technical debt. Most teams don't measure it.
- **Coverage**: the percentage of features that have docs, tours, changelogs, announcements. Most teams estimate 80%. Reality is closer to 40%.
- **The structural asymmetry**: engineering ships continuously (CI/CD). Communication is manual, cross-functional, and always behind.
- **Stale content**: help articles that describe how a feature used to work. Calendar-based freshness checks (flag after 90 days) miss the real trigger:code changes.
- **The coordination tax**: PMs spend 5–10 hours/week on communication coordination that could be automated.
- **The invisible failure mode**: features that ship without announcement. Users discover changes by accident or not at all. Adoption stalls for reasons nobody can diagnose.

## EXAMPLE POSTS

**Example 1:LinkedIn (Misconception Hook):**

Most product teams think their docs are up to date.

Then a support ticket comes in about a feature that changed three weeks ago. The help article still shows the old workflow. The tour still points to a button that moved.

Nobody noticed because nobody's job is to notice.

The gap between "we shipped it" and "users understand it" isn't a people problem. It's a structural one. The information needed to update every channel already exists in the code. It's just not connected to anything.


**Example 2:Twitter (Recognition Hook):**

You shipped 12 features last quarter.
Your changelog mentions 4.
Your help center covers 7.
Your onboarding tour references 3.

That's not a content problem. That's a coverage problem. And most teams don't measure it.


**Example 3:LinkedIn (Number Hook):**

Average help article age across 50 B2B SaaS help centers: 14 months.
Average deploy frequency of those same teams: 2.3x per week.

That's roughly 120 deploys between doc updates.

The docs aren't wrong because teams are lazy. They're wrong because nothing connects code changes to content changes. The trigger is missing.
